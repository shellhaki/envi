Access is granted per environment, at one of three levels:

| Permission | Can do |
|---|---|
| `read` | View and pull secrets |
| `write` | Everything `read` can, plus push and change secrets |
| `manage` | Everything `write` can, plus invite others and manage access |

Each level includes the ones before it — `write` implies `read`, `manage` implies both.

## Who gets access, and why

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Organization owners and admins">
    Always have full access to every project and environment in the organization. This can't be scoped away — it's the account-recovery path.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Any org member, on non-production environments">
    Development and staging environments are open to anyone in the organization by default, no explicit grant required. The idea is that day-to-day environments shouldn't need a manage-level person to hand-provision access for every teammate.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Explicit grants, for everything else">
    Production environments require a real, explicit grant — from an invitation (`envi share`) or a direct grant by someone with `manage` access. There's no ambient access to production through organization membership alone.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

This is the practical meaning of "production stays explicit": being on the team gets you into `development` immediately; getting into `production` is always a deliberate, visible act — either accepting an invitation or being granted access directly, both of which show up in the audit log.

## Service tokens

A [service token](/ci-service-tokens) is scoped exactly like a human grant — one environment, one permission level — but it isn't tied to any person's session. Revoking it doesn't sign anyone out; it just stops that one credential from working.

## Checking what's granted

```bash
envi activity
```

shows recent reads and writes across the org, including which identity — human or service token — touched what.
